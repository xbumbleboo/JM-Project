# Methodology appendix — JM Synthetic Data

> **Synthetic data.** All data in this project was generated by the author in Python, based on JM's public scale, policies, prices and promotions. These are not JM's real figures and do not reflect JM's actual business performance.

*Version of 14 November 2026 · Companion to [#3 Insight report](insight-report.md)*

This document shows how the conclusions of [#3](insight-report.md) were reached and tested, on the same dataset and queries.

**Principles.** (1) Call a difference real only when it clearly exceeds noise; when comparing many groups, first count how many would cross the threshold by chance. Not crossing the threshold means **insufficient evidence**, not proof that groups are identical. (2) Measure at the right unit: gross margin is compared across 600 styles, not 7,213 SKUs, because cost is assigned per style. (3) State the denominator of every rate. (4) Test methods on data where the answer is known (section 7).

The summary table places each conclusion at one of three levels: **Verified** (unchanged under reasonable alternative calculations), **Conditional** (holds within the stated assumptions), **Insufficient evidence**.

## 1. Decomposing the change in revenue

*[#3](insight-report.md) section [2.1](insight-report.md#21-fewer-purchases-offset-by-price) · Did revenue fall because of fewer purchases or lower value per purchase?*

Revenue = transaction count (N) × value per transaction (A). Chain substitution: part due to transaction count = (N₁ − N₀) × A₀; part due to value = N₁ × (A₁ − A₀); the two add up exactly to the total change. The result depends on the order of substitution, so it was recomputed four ways (Jan–Aug 2026 against the same period of 2025, VND bn):

| Decomposition | Due to transaction count | Due to value per transaction |
|---|---:|---:|
| Transaction count first (used in [#3](insight-report.md)) | −21.53 | +17.39 |
| Value first | −23.54 | +19.40 |
| Midpoint of the two orders | −22.53 | +18.39 |
| LMDI (logarithmic split) | −22.50 | +18.36 |

The conclusion does not change: value per transaction offsets 81–82% of the loss from transaction count. Transaction count is not split further into customers × frequency, because section 5 shows that split is purely mechanical.

## 2. Promotion effectiveness: daily quasi-Poisson regression

*[#3](insight-report.md) section [4](insight-report.md#4-promotion-effectiveness--what-do-promotions-deliver) · How many extra invoices and how much extra revenue does each programme bring compared with baseline days?*

**Model** over 974 days (1 January 2024–31 August 2026): `log E[offline sales invoices] = year + month + weekend + programme`. A baseline day has no programme. Overlapping days go to the programme with the larger effect (13–15 July to JM Anniversary, 16–17 July to the Mid-month Sale 15/7). "New collection launch" covers all of October–December, so Black Friday is measured against that baseline. The same model is run on sales-invoice revenue and on online orders.

**Reading the coefficients.** Uplift = e^β − 1. The 95% confidence interval is computed on the log scale: [e^(β − 1.96·SE) − 1 ; e^(β + 1.96·SE) − 1]; [#3](insight-report.md) writes it compactly as ± (delta-method approximation). Each dependent variable has 7 pooled coefficients (main model) and 20 year-specific ones (interaction model, excluding Black Friday 2026); across all 54 intervals the two approaches differ by at most 0.6 points and no conclusion changes. Change in AOV = (1 + revenue uplift) ÷ (1 + invoice uplift) − 1; for example, Hot Sale 8/3: 1.162 ÷ 1.442 − 1 = −19.4%.

**Why this method.** The log form makes effects multiply, as the business thinks ("weekends sell about 1.35 times an ordinary day"), and one model separates seasonality, weekends and overlapping programmes. **Quasi-Poisson** because the data is more dispersed than Poisson (dispersion factor 1.48 for invoices, 2.77 for revenue); without it, confidence intervals would be artificially narrow. For revenue (a continuous variable), the coefficients are consistent when the mean is correctly specified; the standard errors additionally assume variance proportional to the mean.

**How it was tested.** Programme × year interaction (13 extra parameters): p = 0.28 (invoices), 0.36 (revenue), so [#3](insight-report.md) uses pooled coefficients. Online order uplift has the same sign and size as offline for all 7 programmes. Each programme covers 6–96 days; the Mid-month Sale 15/7 has the fewest, hence the widest interval (±6.1). Recovering the built-in coefficients: section 7.

**Limitation.** Uplift is relative to baseline days, **not** incremental revenue: customers may simply shift their purchase date.

## 3. Differences between groups: real or noise?

*[#3](insight-report.md) sections [2.3](insight-report.md#23-by-province-channel-and-product-line), [3.1](insight-report.md#31-by-store-scale-and-spending-power), [5.2](insight-report.md#52-return-reasons-and-the-online-product-mix), [6](insight-report.md#6-products--where-do-sales-come-from-within-the-range) · Is a store, product line or collection that differs from the overall level genuinely different, or is it chance?*

Each group's deviation is measured in standard errors (z). With a threshold of |z| > 2, about 4.6% of groups cross it even when nothing differs (about 1.3 of 29 stores). The four tests rest on different error assumptions, so each has its own robustness check.

### 3.1 Year-on-year change by store

Invoice counts per year are assumed Poisson. Expected 2026 = the store's 2025 invoices × the chain-wide decline k; z = (n₂₀₂₆ − expected) ÷ √(n₂₀₂₆ + n₂₀₂₅ × k²).

- 3/29 stores exceed 2 standard errors (largest |z| 2.69), against about 1.3 by chance. Overall test: the sum of z² against χ² on 28 degrees of freedom (one subtracted because k is estimated from the same stores), p ≈ 0.08 in 2026, 0.18 in 2025. This is an approximation under the Poisson assumption; the p-values are indicative only.
- Sensitivity: dividing z by the square root of the dispersion factor estimated from the same 29 stores (1.40 in 2026, 1.23 in 2025) leaves 1/29 and 0/29 stores above the threshold.
- A store's 2025 and 2026 changes correlate at −0.66: a real decline would move in the same direction twice; a negative correlation is a sign of noise.

There is insufficient evidence that any store declined differently from the chain, so [#3](insight-report.md) does not rank stores by growth.

### 3.2 AOV by store

Regressing log AOV for 29 stores × 3 years on market tier, shopping mall, city centre, Hai Phong and year: tier 2 is about 12% below tier 1, tier 3 about 24%; shopping-mall stores are about 6% higher, city-centre stores about 4%; Hai Phong is about 28% below stores in the same tier. These characteristics explain 96.1% of AOV differences between stores (81.1% without the Hai Phong indicator, which rests on only 2 stores).

Three years of the same store are not independent, so further checks were run: a two-way analysis of variance, store × year, F(28, 56) = 333; store-clustered standard errors (1.3–1.8 times larger) still leave all 5 characteristics significant, joint Wald test F(5, 28) = 1,046; a one-row-per-store regression gives the same result. The weakest coefficient is city centre (p = 0.012 with clustered standard errors, 0.017 with the one-row-per-store regression), so the +4% is best read as a tendency.

### 3.3 Return reasons by channel

A table of 7 channels × 5 reasons (6,408 returned orders), with each cell's expected count based on the online-wide reason shares. 0/35 cells exceed 2 standard errors (against about 1.6 by chance). Overall χ² test: p = 0.86; a permutation test with 20,000 permutations keeping the channel and reason totals fixed (because some cells have expected counts below 5): p = 0.85; Cramér's V = 0.03. There is insufficient evidence that the mix of return reasons differs between channels; channels do differ clearly in the *level* of returns.

### 3.4 Dead SKUs against sales speed

A dead SKU sold no units in the last 30 days of the period. Raw probability pᵢ = exp(−lines sold in the previous 365 days × 30/365), for all 7,213 SKUs; multiplied by a common factor k so that total expected E equals total actual (k = 2.99 at the 2025 snapshot, 2.01 at 2026). Scaling by k is a pragmatic calibration (August is a low season and sales speed is uneven), so the model is used only to compare groups. The z used in the results below follows the Poisson approximation: (actual − E) ÷ √E. As a sensitivity check, the exact variance of a sum of 0/1 variables, E − k² × Σpᵢ² (every k × pᵢ ≤ 0.38), changes z by at most 4% and changes no group's conclusion against the threshold of 2.

Product lines: none has |z| > 2 in 2026 (2025: Accessories +2.07); overall test p = 0.38 (2026) and 0.35 (2025). Collections: 18/20 are within 2 standard errors. TẾT is clearly above (19 against 6.6; z = +4.82) because in August it sells only 63.7% of its own average (other collections 80.1%); adjusting for seasonality, its expected count is about 16, z ≈ 0.7. STREET deviates the other way (z = −2.16), which can happen when comparing 20 groups.

## 4. Testing the churn label on a fixed customer cohort

*[#3](insight-report.md) section [7.2](insight-report.md#72-the-real-dividing-line-one-time-and-repeat-buyers) · Does an "at risk of churning" label tell which customers will not come back?*

**The label.** Silence ratio = days since the last purchase ÷ purchase interval, where purchase interval = days from first purchase to the snapshot date ÷ number of repeat purchases. Repeat buyers (2 or more purchase days) are placed into "buying regularly" (below 1), "at risk" (1–2) and "lapsed" (above 2).

**Design.** 35,638 identified customers with a purchase day up to 31 August 2025, labelled at that date and followed for 12 months; "came back" = at least one purchase day with revenue.

| Group at 31 August 2025 | Customers | Came back within 12 months | Revenue per customer, 12 months |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Repeat buyers, buying regularly | 18,090 | 85.4% | VND 9.29m |
| Repeat buyers, "at risk" | 7,079 | 92.3% | VND 11.00m |
| Repeat buyers, "lapsed" | 3,514 | 98.0% | VND 15.86m |
| Bought only once so far¹ | 6,955 | 37.8% | VND 0.75m |

*¹ [#3](insight-report.md) reports a range, 36.4–38.3% and VND 0.72–0.76m: two subgroups whose first purchase fell within 189 days of the snapshot (1,756 customers) or earlier (5,199).*

**Composition effect.** The "lapsed" group comes back more often because it contains many frequent buyers. Within the same number of repeat purchases, the three labels are almost identical (11–20 repeat purchases: 99.7% in all three).

**A flaw in the formula, true on any data.** With T = days from first purchase to the snapshot date and f = number of repeat purchases: silence ratio = f × (days of silence ÷ T) ≤ f. A customer who has come back 1–2 times has a ratio of at most 2, so they can **never** reach "lapsed" (above 2). A suggested fix, not tested in this project: purchase interval = days between first and last purchase ÷ f, so the silent period is no longer counted inside the purchase interval (T currently includes it). A revised label would still need to pass the same test before use.

This dataset has no churn mechanism, so the return rates say nothing about real-world retention; what carries over to real data is the test and the formula flaw.

## 5. Fixed-customer simulation: what does a 49% fall in new customers mean?

*[#3](insight-report.md) section [7.1](insight-report.md#71-slightly-fewer-customers-but-each-buys-less-often) · Does a halving of new customers show that acquisition has weakened?*

A "new customer" is one appearing in the data for the first time (the data starts on 1 January 2024), so later periods have fewer "new" customers even when nothing changes. The test **checks a mechanism in the data**, not the real number of customers: if no new customers ever arrive, do "new customers" still fall?

Setup: 75,000 customers present from the start, each with a fixed Gamma purchase weight (shape 0.18, mean 1), exactly the two parameters of the data generator, not tuned to this test. Each draw picks a customer in proportion to weight, with replacement; the number of draws equals the actual identified-customer transactions, drawn in 5 periods from Jan–Aug 2024 (202,848), Sep–Dec 2024 (125,729), Jan–Aug 2025 (164,248), Sep–Dec 2025 (102,253) to Jan–Aug 2026 (147,727). 20 runs (seed 2026); ± is the standard deviation across runs.

| | Actual¹ | Simulated (no new customers) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Customers, Jan–Aug 2026 | 27,135 | 26,988 ± 133 |
| New customers, Jan–Aug 2025 → 2026 | 2,921 → 1,489 (−49.0%) | 2,867 → 1,447 (−49.5%) |

*¹ Counted the same way as the simulation: customers with at least one transaction in the period. The dashboard KPIs count customers with a purchase day with revenue (26,827 customers; 1,506 new customers, −48.9%); the 308-customer gap consists of customers whose transactions in the period all had zero revenue (fully returned orders).*

Over 200 runs: average fall −49.3%, 2.5%–97.5% percentile range −52.5% to −46.2%; customers 26,819–27,223. Both actual figures lie within these ranges. "New customers −48.9%" is therefore **not enough** to conclude that acquisition has weakened.

## 6. CLV: BG/NBD and Gamma-Gamma

*[#3](insight-report.md) section [7.3](insight-report.md#73-revenue-concentration-by-customer-decile) · Which customers will be most valuable over the next 12 months?*

**Model.** BG/NBD forecasts the number of purchases; Gamma-Gamma forecasts value per purchase, assuming it does not depend on purchase count. 12-month CLV = Σ (month i = 1…12) [value per purchase × forecast purchases in month i ÷ 1.01^i]; the 1% monthly discount lowers absolute levels by about 6% and barely changes the ranking. Input: 706,121 purchase days from 38,446 identified customers up to 31 August 2026.

**Calibration/hold-out test.** Fitted on 2024–2025, forecasting Jan–Aug 2026 for the 36,940 customers who had purchased before the hold-out:

| Ability | Metric | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Level forecasting | Forecast / actual purchase days | 187,593 / 139,905 (34.1% too high) |
| Ranking | Spearman: BG/NBD · past purchase count · days of silence | 0.808 · **0.832** · 0.624 |
| Ranking | Lift, top 10% against bottom 10% | 57.6×; no decile out of order |
| Gamma-Gamma assumption | Correlation of purchase count × value per purchase | +0.04 (2026), +0.08 (2025) |

**Reading the results.**
- *The level forecast is wrong, with a consistent explanation:* system-wide purchase intensity fell 24.5% (from 772.1 to 583.0 purchase days per calendar day), while BG/NBD assumes a constant rate; this alone corresponds to a forecast about 32% too high (1 ÷ 0.755). The rest comes from 1,193 single-purchase customers at the end of calibration (forecast 2,427 purchase days, actual 375); excluding them, the forecast is 32.7% too high. The hold-out covers only January–August, so the fall includes seasonality.
- *Ranking is good but no better than ranking by past purchase count.*
- *The churn parameter is almost unidentifiable* (moving a from 0.0009 to 0.0023 changes the log-likelihood by 8 out of about 2.2 million), so the probability of being "alive" is not used.
- *The Gamma-Gamma assumption is supported* (correlation below 0.1), not proven.

**Management conclusion.** The model **has not shown added value over ranking by purchase count**, so there is no basis yet for using it to allocate budget; [#3](insight-report.md) uses CLV only to describe the concentration of forecast value.

**Implementation check.** With numpy 2.x, the `lifetimes` library silently returned empty values for 1,193 customers, so the first run reported an over-forecast of 32.35% instead of 34.1%. The model was rewritten in scipy following Fader, Hardie & Lee (2005), tested on the CDNOW dataset (reproducing the published parameters: r 0.243 · α 4.414 · a 0.793 · b 2.426) and compared with the original CLV table (Spearman 0.9998; `models/clv_model.py`).

## 7. What can synthetic data test?

Data generated from rules allows a test real data cannot: **does the method recover what was built in, and avoid finding what is not there?** The promotion model recovered all 7 built-in coefficients within their 95% confidence intervals (pooled 2024–2026):

| Programme | Built-in coefficient | Measured uplift |
|---|---:|---:|
| Hot Sale 8/3 | +45% | +44.2% ±3.5 |
| Black Friday (against the "New collection launch" baseline) | 1.45 ÷ 1.10 = +31.8% | +32.2% ±2.7 |
| Year-end Clearance & Spring | +30% | +30.2% ±3.2 |
| JM Anniversary | +25% | +23.2% ±2.3 |
| 30/4 – 1/5 holiday | +25% | +23.0% ±2.8 |
| Mid-month Sale 15/7 | +20% | +18.0% ±6.1 |
| End of Season | +15% | +15.6% ±2.3 |

By year, 19/20 cells contain the built-in coefficient (the exception: 30/4 – 1/5 holiday in 2024, +19.5% ±4.3), as expected with 95% intervals. This is **a single generated dataset** (seed 42), not a coverage study. The churn label (section 4) and the fall in new customers (section 5) look like real signals but are both explained by other mechanisms. Recovering the coefficients only shows the method is correct **when the model matches how the data was generated**.

| Comparison with noise: tests that found no difference | 2.3, 3.1, 5.2, 6 | No difference beyond noise detected (except TẾT, seasonal) | Insufficient evidence of a difference | Ranking by growth; groups being identical |
| Fixed-cohort test | 7.2 | The churn label adds nothing beyond purchase count; the formula has a flaw | Verified | Real-world retention rates |

| BG/NBD + Gamma-Gamma | 7.3 | Ranking usable, on a par with ranking by purchase count | Conditional | The model beating the baseline; absolute values |

## Applying it to a real business

| Method | What to do |
|---|---|
| Revenue decomposition | State the substitution order or use LMDI; split further by channel and product mix |
| Promotion uplift | Add lunar holidays, weather, ad spend; measure incremental revenue with a control group of stores (difference-in-differences) |
| Comparison with noise | Set alert thresholds in standard errors, estimating variation from historical data; check the previous year (real problems repeat, noise reverses); exclude out-of-season collections from dead-SKU alerts |
| Churn label | Label at a past date, look 12 months ahead, compare within the same purchase count, before spending budget |
| New customers | Define with a look-back window (for example, no purchase in 24 months) |
| CLV | Run a hold-out test, measure level forecasting and ranking separately, always compare with ranking by purchase count |

## Glossary

**Baseline day:** a day with no promotion, the reference for uplift. **Dispersion factor:** Pearson χ² ÷ degrees of freedom; 1 if the data is exactly Poisson. **LMDI:** a logarithmic decomposition that does not depend on substitution order. **Clustered standard errors:** allow for observations from the same store not being independent. **Decile lift:** ratio of actual value between the top-ranked 10% and the bottom-ranked 10%.
